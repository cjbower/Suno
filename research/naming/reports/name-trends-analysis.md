# Name Trends Analysis

**Purpose**

This analysis synthesizes patterns across the decade-based first-name datasets to identify naming trends, generational shifts, and implications for artist branding.

**Sources**

- 1970s-2020s decade datasets (male and female)
- U.S. Social Security Administration birth records
- Census-based frequency analysis

---

## KEY FINDINGS

### Finding 1: THE GREAT NAMING DIVIDE (2010s Inflection Point)

**Observation:** The 2010s mark a dramatic generational break in naming preferences.

**Male Names – Before 2010s (1970s-2000s):**
- Michael dominated #1 for 40 consecutive years
- Jason, Robert, James consistently top 4
- Traditional/biblical names rule
- Stability: Top 20 nearly identical across four decades

**Male Names – After 2010s (2010s-2020s):**
- Liam, Noah, Oliver are NEW top names
- Michael drops to #12 (from #1 for 40 years)
- Jason, Robert completely absent from top 20
- Contemporary/literary names rule
- Radical shift in naming preferences

**Female Names – Before 2010s (1970s-2000s):**
- Jennifer dominated #1 for 30 consecutive years
- Maria, Mary consistently top 2-3
- Traditional/biblical names rule
- Extreme stability: Top 20 identical from 1970s-2000s

**Female Names – After 2010s (2010s-2020s):**
- Olivia, Emma, Ava are NEW top names
- Jennifer completely absent from top 20 (dropped from #1)
- Maria, Mary completely absent from top 20
- Contemporary/literary names rule
- Complete generational break from previous patterns

**Implication:** This represents the most significant generational naming shift in modern U.S. history. The transition occurred around 2008-2010.

### Finding 2: GENERATIONAL NAME RECOGNITION

**Generational Familiarity by Name:**

**Gen-X/Millennial Names (Familiar to 40+ and 25-50 year-olds):**
- Michael, Jason, Robert, James (male)
- Jennifer, Maria, Mary (female)
- These names were dominant 1970s-2000s
- Recognition: Extremely high in this demographic

**Gen-Z/Gen-Alpha Names (Familiar to under 25):**
- Liam, Noah, Oliver, Elijah (male)
- Olivia, Emma, Ava, Isabella (female)
- These names became dominant after 2008
- Recognition: Extremely high in this demographic

**Cross-Generational Transition Names:**
- William, James, Benjamin (male) – appear in both eras
- Emily, Elizabeth (female) – appear in both eras

**Implication:** Artist naming should consider target audience age range. Gen-X targeting benefits from traditional names; Gen-Z targeting benefits from contemporary names.

### Finding 3: RISING AND DECLINING NAMES

**Rising Names (Increasing rank position over time):**

Male:
- Christopher (rank 11, 1970s → 10, 2010s – remained stable but gained prominence)
- Matthew (rank 16, 1970s → 20, 2010s – stable mid-range)
- Joshua (not top 20, 1970s → rank 19, 1990s – dramatic rise)
- Liam (not top 20, 2000s → rank 1, 2010s – meteoric rise)
- Noah (rank unknown, 2000s → rank 2, 2010s – dramatic rise)
- Oliver (rank unknown, 2000s → rank 3, 2010s – dramatic rise)

Female:
- Emily (rank 13-14, 1990s-2000s → rank 12, 2010s – steady climb)
- Isabella (rank unknown → rank 4, 2010s – dramatic rise)
- Olivia (rank unknown → rank 1, 2010s – meteoric rise)
- Emma (rank unknown → rank 2, 2010s – dramatic rise)
- Ava (rank unknown → rank 3, 2010s – dramatic rise)

**Declining Names (Decreasing from previous prominence):**

Male:
- Michael (rank 1, 1970s-2000s → rank 12, 2010s – dramatic decline)
- Jason (rank 2, 1970s-1990s → not top 20, 2010s – eliminated from top 20)
- Robert (rank 3, 1970s-2000s → not top 20, 2010s – eliminated from top 20)
- David (rank 6, 1970s-2000s → not top 20, 2010s – eliminated from top 20)

Female:
- Jennifer (rank 1, 1970s-2000s → not top 20, 2010s – completely disappeared)
- Maria (rank 2, 1970s-2000s → not top 20, 2010s – completely disappeared)
- Mary (rank 3, 1970s-2000s → not top 20, 2010s – eliminated from top 20)
- Susan (rank 4, 1970s-2000s → not top 20, 2010s – eliminated)

**Implication:** Rising names have contemporary cachet; declining names feel dated to Gen-Z audiences but carry Gen-X/Millennial recognition.

### Finding 5: CULTURAL/ETHNIC NAME PATTERNS

**English/Northern European Surnames + Names:**
- Dominant 1970s-2000s
- Decline 2010s+
- Still popular but less dominant

**Spanish/Hispanic Names:**
- Increasing representation in birth records
- Garcia, Rodriguez, Martinez rise in surnames
- Spanish first names less documented in census data but increasingly popular
- Cultural significance for Latin/Latin-influenced music

**Literary/Contemporary Names:**
- Emerging 2010s+
- Often drawn from literature, pop culture, mythology
- Liam (from mythology), Oliver (Dickens/Shakespeare), Elijah (biblical but contemporary context)
- Olivia (Shakespeare), Scarlett (pop culture), Harper (contemporary)

**Implication:** Artists with Spanish surnames naturally fit Latin/bilingual content. Contemporary names (2010s+) appeal to Gen-Z audiences.

## NAMING OBSERVATIONS FOR STUDIO STRATEGY

### Observation 1: Generation-Targeted Naming

**Gen-X Audience Targeting (40+ years old):**
- Use names from 1970s-1980s datasets
- Michael, Jason, Robert, James (male) have immediate recognition
- Jennifer, Maria, Susan (female) have immediate recognition
- Recommendation: Use for nostalgia/retro-appeal content

**Gen-Z/Millennial Audience Targeting (Under 35 years old):**
- Use names from 2010s-2020s datasets
- Liam, Noah, Oliver, Elijah (male) have contemporary appeal
- Olivia, Emma, Ava (female) have contemporary appeal
- Recommendation: Use for contemporary/modern content

**Cross-Generational Targeting:**
- Use names that appear in both eras: William, James, Benjamin (male)
- Emily, Elizabeth, Abigail (female)
- Lower collision risk with distinctive surnames
- Recommendation: Safest choice for broad appeal

### Observation 2: Collision Risk vs. Contemporary Appeal

**High Collision Risk Names (Familiar but crowded):**
- Michael, Jason, Robert, James
- Jennifer, Maria, Mary
- Extreme searchability disadvantage
- BUT: High generational recognition

**Low Collision Risk Names (Contemporary but trendy):**
- Liam, Noah, Oliver, Elijah
- Olivia, Emma, Ava
- Excellent searchability advantage
- BUT: May feel dated in 10-15 years

**Mid-Range Sweet Spot:**
- William, Benjamin, Alexander (male)
- Charlotte, Amelia, Harper (female)
- Moderate collision risk
- Strong contemporary appeal
- Good longevity potential

### Observation 3: First Name + Surname Interaction

**Most Difficult Combinations (highest collision risk):**
- Michael Smith (literally millions of matches)
- Jennifer Johnson (literally millions of matches)
- Robert Williams
- Mary Brown
- James Jones

**Moderate Difficulty:**
- Michael Garcia, Michael Rodriguez (medium-high collision)
- Jennifer Williams, Jennifer Davis
- These benefit from non-English surname

**Best Searchability:**
- Michael Reyes, Michael Vega (clear identity)
- Jennifer Martinez, Jennifer Cruz (clear identity)
- Lucas Reed, Lucas Grant (clear identity)
- Olivia Brooks, Sophia Martinez (clear identity)

## IMPLICATIONS FOR SUNO STUDIO

### For Current Artist Roster

Artists with Spanish surnames (Reyes, Cross, Vega, Navarro, Cruz, Martinez) have natural advantage:
- Lower collision risk when combined with any first name
- Clear cultural/linguistic branding
- Distinctive online presence
- Good for Latin/bilingual content strategy

### For Current Band Names

Band names bypass first-name collision issues entirely. They are more differentiated by nature. No collision risk from naming patterns.

### For Future Naming Work

1. **Understand generational targets:** Gen-X vs. Gen-Z audiences respond to different naming patterns
2. **Prioritize searchability:** Avoid extreme collision combinations
3. **Consider cultural fit:** Spanish surnames naturally support Latin content
4. **Plan for longevity:** Contemporary names (2010s+) may feel dated in 10 years; classic names (William, James) remain timeless
5. **Use surnames strategically:** Choose surnames that support distinctiveness and cultural positioning

## RESEARCH LIMITATIONS

This analysis is based on:
- U.S. Social Security Administration birth records (English-language dominant)
- Census data from 2010 (most recent comprehensive)
- English-language naming preferences
- Primarily American-born populations

This analysis does NOT cover:
- International naming patterns
- Ethnic/cultural naming traditions outside English-language databases
- Hyphenated or compound names
- Surname-first naming patterns (common in many cultures)
- Regional naming variations
- Recent immigrant naming patterns (2010-2024)
