# Name Selection Strategy

**Purpose**

This document establishes principles and methodology for naming decisions at Suno Studio. It provides decision-making guidance without prescribing specific names.

**Scope**

This strategy applies to:
- Future artist roster expansions
- Future band recruitment
- Producer projects
- Other studio initiatives requiring naming

This strategy does NOT prescribe:
- Specific name changes (covered in separate phases)
- Renaming decisions for existing artists/bands
- Personal identity decisions (names are personal)

---

## GUIDING PRINCIPLES

### Principle 1: Names Should Be Familiar

**Definition:** Names should be recognizable to target audiences, not obscure or difficult to pronounce.

**Rationale:**
- Familiarity aids word-of-mouth discovery
- Recognizable names reduce cognitive load
- Audiences can easily remember and recommend

**How to Apply:**
1. Use names that appear in naming datasets (1970s-2020s)
2. Avoid extremely obscure, made-up, or artificially constructed names
3. Prefer names with linguistic/cultural roots
4. Test for pronunciation clarity across diverse audiences

**Practical Examples:**
- Good: Adrian, Nathan, Sophia, Oliver (documented in SSA datasets)
- Avoid: Zxplyx, Kymbi, Thyron (made-up, unfamiliar)

---

### Principle 2: Names Should Be Believable

**Definition:** Names should feel authentic, not corporate-branded or artificially constructed.

**Rationale:**
- Audiences connect with authentic artists
- Believable names build artist credibility
- Artificial names create distance from audiences

**How to Apply:**
1. Use real names or name-based identities (not corporate constructs)
2. Avoid names that feel like marketing terms
3. Prefer traditional patronymic/occupational structures
4. Test for authenticity/legitimacy with beta audiences

**Practical Examples:**
- Good: "Elena Cruz" (authentic personal name structure)
- Good: "Lantern House" (poetic but authentic band concept)
- Avoid: "MusicFlow™ Elite" (corporate branding)
- Avoid: "GenZ.Vibes.AI" (artificial/trendy construct)

---

### Principle 3: Names Should Be Memorable

**Definition:** Names should be easy to remember and repeat, with minimal cognitive friction.

**Rationale:**
- Memory is prerequisite for word-of-mouth discovery
- Memorable names reduce friction in artist searches
- Shorter recall time means increased discoverability

**How to Apply:**
1. Prefer 2-3 word combinations (name + surname)
2. Avoid complex syllable structures
3. Test for phone/voice retention (can someone say it once and remember it?)
4. Use rhyming or alliterative structures cautiously (can enhance or harm memorability)

**Practical Examples:**
- Good: "Lucas Reed" (2 syllables + 1 syllable = easy recall)
- Good: "Signal Fire" (2 syllables + 1 syllable = easy recall)
- Avoid: "Cornelius Beauregard Thaddeus Van Dyke" (too complex)
- Avoid: "Zephyr Eclipse Meridian" (too ethereal/difficult to recall)

---

### Principle 4: Names Should Be Searchable

**Definition:** Names should be easy to find and distinguish online through search engines and streaming platforms.

**Rationale:**
- Online discoverability is prerequisite for modern music careers
- Unclear names create friction in audience acquisition
- Searchability directly correlates with listenership

**How to Apply:**
1. Avoid extremely common combinations (Michael Smith, Jennifer Johnson)
2. Use combination of familiar + distinctive elements
3. Assess collision risk using surname/first-name datasets
4. Test search engine performance (does the name resolve to the intended artist?)
5. Verify availability on major platforms (Spotify, Apple Music, YouTube)

**Practical Examples:**
- Good: "Adrian Santiago" (Adrian familiar, Reyes distinctive) = good searchability
- Good: "Signal Fire" (simple, clear, unique) = excellent searchability
- Avoid: "Michael Smith" (too common, lost in results)
- Avoid: "Jennifer Johnson" (too common, lost in results)

---

### Principle 5: Names Should Be Brandable

**Definition:** Names should support and reinforce visual/creative branding identity.

**Rationale:**
- Brand identity extends beyond music to imagery, visual design, aesthetic
- Names that support branding are more effective marketing assets
- Visual artists benefit when name reinforces artistic direction

**How to Apply:**
1. Assess thematic/visual potential (does name suggest imagery, color, era, genre?)
2. Test for logo/visual potential (can the name be visualized?)
3. Consider cultural branding (does name support targeted cultural positioning?)
4. Use naming to signal genre/audience expectations

**Practical Examples:**
- Good: "Lantern House" (warm imagery, intimate aesthetic, folk branding)
- Good: "Harbor Lights" (maritime/atmospheric imagery, scenic potential)
- Good: "Elena Cruz" (Spanish/Latin cultural branding)
- Avoid: "BrandName42" (no visual potential, corporate feel)

---

### Principle 6: Names Should Have Low Collision Risk

**Definition:** Names should be uncommon enough to establish unique online/industry identity.

**Rationale:**
- High collision risk means lost searchability
- Competing artists with same name create confusion
- Low collision means stronger brand differentiation

**How to Apply:**
1. Use collision analysis from [Current Artist Review](../industry-collision-analysis/current-artist-review.md)
2. Use collision analysis from [Current Band Review](../industry-collision-analysis/current-band-review.md)
3. Avoid top-5 most common name combinations
4. Combine common first names with less-common surnames
5. Use Spanish surnames (statistically lower collision risk in English-language music databases)
6. Assess band name uniqueness (band names have inherently lower collision than individual names)

**Practical Examples:**
- Good: "Adrian Santiago" (common first name + distinctive Spanish surname)
- Good: "Nathan Cross" (familiar first name + rare surname)
- Avoid: "Michael Smith" (top 1 collision risk)
- Avoid: "Jennifer Johnson" (top-5 collision risk)

**Collision Risk Guidance:**
- LOW: Unique combination, good searchability, strong differentiation
- MEDIUM: Some matches, requires marketing differentiation
- HIGH: Multiple matches, requires aggressive brand positioning
- VERY HIGH: Avoid unless compelling artistic reason supersedes searchability

---

### Principle 7: Emotional Authenticity Supersedes Branding Purity

**Definition:** Authenticity and emotional connection matter more than perfect naming formula.

**Rationale:**
- Audiences connect with authentic artists, not optimized personas
- Artistic integrity is prerequisite for long-term career success
- Over-engineering names creates distance from audiences
- Creative Constitution Principle #6: Emotional authenticity is more important than genre purity

**How to Apply:**
1. If artist has established personal name, honor it (don't rename for optimization)
2. Consider personal/cultural identity (names carry personal significance)
3. Optimize only when multiple options exist
4. Use naming strategy as guidance, not prescription
5. Respect artist agency in naming decisions

**Practical Examples:**
- Good: Keep "Elena Cruz" if that's the artist's authentic identity
- Good: Accept "Sophia Martinez" despite medium-high collision (emotional authenticity wins)
- Avoid: Renaming "Elena" to "Alejandra" just for SEO
- Avoid: Requiring artists to change family names for branding

## NAMING STRATEGY BY CONTEXT

### For Individual Artists

**Optimal Formula:**
- Familiar first name + Distinctive surname
- Results in Low-to-Medium collision risk with good searchability

**Preferred Patterns:**
1. **Traditional First Name + Spanish Surname** (Excellent for Latin/bilingual content)
   - Examples: Adrian Santiago, Elena Cruz, Sophia Martinez
   - Collision Risk: LOW-TO-MEDIUM
   - Searchability: GOOD-TO-EXCELLENT

2. **Contemporary First Name + Rare Surname** (Excellent for contemporary content)
   - Examples: Lucas Reed, Olivia Brooks, Nathan Cross
   - Collision Risk: LOW
   - Searchability: EXCELLENT

3. **Established Personal Names** (Honor authenticity over optimization)
   - Use artist's authentic identity
   - Optimize only supplementary branding
   - Examples: Respect existing artist identities

**Patterns to Avoid:**
- Extreme collision combinations (Michael Smith, Jennifer Johnson)
- Made-up or artificially constructed names
- Names that feel corporate rather than personal
- Names requiring constant explanation

### For Band Names

**Optimal Formula:**
- Descriptive compound (noun + noun)
- Thematically aligned with music genre
- Poetic/evocative rather than literal

**Preferred Patterns:**
1. **Atmospheric Descriptive** (Good for indie/alternative)
   - Examples: Signal Fire, Harbor Lights, Lantern House
   - Collision Risk: LOW-TO-MEDIUM
   - Searchability: GOOD-TO-EXCELLENT

2. **Inspirational/Spiritual** (Good for Americana/Christian content)
   - Examples: North Star Revival, City Saints
   - Collision Risk: LOW-TO-MEDIUM
   - Searchability: GOOD

3. **Urban/Contemporary** (Good for pop/alternative)
   - Examples: Midnight Avenue
   - Collision Risk: LOW-TO-MEDIUM
   - Searchability: EXCELLENT

**Patterns to Avoid:**
- Generic terms (The Rock Band, Music Factory)
- Trendy slang (will feel dated quickly)
- Inside jokes (alienates new audiences)
- Overly complex structures

### For Producer Projects

**Principles:**
- Treat like band names (descriptive, thematic)
- Signal creative direction (Meridian = navigation/exploration)
- Support multiple artist collaborations
- Avoid proprietary/corporate feel

**Examples:**
- Meridian – implies exploration, navigation, creative direction
- Foundry – implies creation, forging, studio craft

## NAMING BY GENERATION/AUDIENCE

### For Gen-X Audiences (40+ years old)

**Optimal First Names:**
- Michael, Robert, James, John, David (from 1970s-2000s datasets)
- Familiar to this demographic (their generation's top names)

**Recommended Surnames:**
- English occupational names (Miller, Taylor, Walker, Carter)
- Spanish surnames (cultural crossover appeal)
- Avoid newest generational names (may feel inauthentic)

**Example:** "David Miller" – authentic Gen-X branding

### For Millennials (25-40 years old)

**Optimal First Names:**
- Jason, Christopher, Matthew (from 1980s-2000s datasets)
- Contemporary but established

**Recommended Surnames:**
- Mixed English/Spanish surnames
- Occupational or descriptive
- Support both indie and mainstream positioning

**Example:** "Lucas Reed" – contemporary Millennial authenticity

### For Gen-Z (Under 25 years old)

**Optimal First Names:**
- Liam, Noah, Oliver, Elijah (from 2010s-2020s datasets)
- Contemporary, trending, natural to this demographic

**Recommended Surnames:**
- Distinctive/rare surnames
- Spanish surnames (cultural authenticity)
- Support contemporary branding

**Example:** "Liam Vega" – Gen-Z contemporary, Spanish surname = good cultural positioning

### For Cross-Generational Appeal

**Optimal First Names:**
- William, James, Benjamin (traditional but timeless)
- Emily, Elizabeth, Charlotte (classic with contemporary appeal)

**Recommended Surnames:**
- Distinctive surnames (high searchability)
- Support multiple genre/audience contexts

**Example:** "Benjamin Brooks" – timeless appeal across generations

## NAMING DECISION FRAMEWORK

### Step 1: Establish Context

- Target audience demographics?
- Primary genre/artistic direction?
- Cultural/linguistic positioning?
- Existing personal name or new artist creation?

### Step 2: Assess Current Options

- Use existing naming datasets (decade-based lists)
- Document all name options
- Assess collision risk for each
- Evaluate searchability

### Step 3: Apply Principles

Review against 7 principles:
1. Familiar? ✓/✗
2. Believable? ✓/✗
3. Memorable? ✓/✗
4. Searchable? ✓/✗
5. Brandable? ✓/✗
6. Low collision? ✓/✗
7. Authentic? ✓/✗

### Step 4: Prioritize

- Principle 7 (Authenticity) is tier-1 priority
- Principles 1-6 are optimization considerations
- If authenticity conflicts with optimization, honor authenticity

### Step 5: Test & Validate

- Search artist/band names on major platforms
- Test memorability with beta audiences
- Assess visual/branding potential
- Verify availability (Spotify, domain, etc.)

### Step 6: Document Decision

- Record chosen name
- Document decision rationale
- Explain how principles were applied
- Note any conflicts/trade-offs

## SPECIAL CONSIDERATIONS

### Spanish Surnames and Latin Content

**Advantage:** Spanish surnames naturally support Latin/bilingual content strategy

**Example Artists in Roster:**
- Adrian Santiago, Isaac Vega, Ethan Navarro (traditional/contemporary + Spanish)
- Elena Cruz, Sophia Martinez (traditional/contemporary + Spanish)
- Isabella Reyes, Natalie Vega, (contemporary + Spanish)

**Recommendation:** Leverage Spanish surnames for Latin/bilingual positioning. These provide natural cultural branding advantage.

### Unisex Names and Branding Flexibility

**Example:** Jordan Mercer (unisex name, branding flexibility)

**Advantage:** Unisex names support diverse branding interpretations and audience reach

**Consideration:** Requires clear visual/marketing branding to establish artist identity

### Made-Up Names and Artistic Direction

**Question:** When might artists choose made-up names?

**Answer:** Only when artistic vision requires it, and authentication/authenticity strategy is clear.

**Examples:**
- Stage personas with established artistic integrity (musician creates alter-ego for specific genre)
- Artistic collectives with invented identity
- When cultural/personal identity conflicts with industry norms and artist chooses artistic intervention

**Principle:** Made-up names are exception, not norm. Reserve for specific artistic reasons with established authenticity markers.
